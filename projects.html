High Accuracy Stream Mapping

Streams play a key role in the water cycle, transporting water from the land to the oceans and helping regulate Earth’s climate. The stream map is a fundamental dataset for hydrology and geomorphology research. The cornerstone of stream mapping is an object recognition task, which extracts the stream objects from terrain data. However, there are many challenges: the terrain data include heterogeneous noise, streams of varied shapes, and objects resembling streams that are not streams. Beyond the object recognition, the mapping tasks also include reasoning the existence of indistinct shaped streams to build an integrated stream network and computing stream water directions.

My current research mainly focuses on automatically mapping streams in the Chesapeake Bay watershed (CBW), which spans more than 64,000 square miles, using 1-meter resolution DEM. I collaborated with my postdoctoral supervisor, Professor Matthew Baker, to develop an innovative approach. This method utilizes the geomorphon (a morphology-based algorithm) to uncover the potential stream objects, the random forest model to filter the stream objects, and proximity analysis and surface flow routing methods to reason the stream without distinct shapes and to calculate the water flow direction. I implemented a program using open-source GIS libraries and deployed it on UMBC’s HPC cluster.

Specifically, my contributions include three parts: 1) I architected the whole program and developed an HPC task assignment program, which enhances data processing reliability, optimizes processing efficiency and fully automates the process (This algorithm can detect and bypass unreliable nodes while allocating tasks based on the availability of idle HPC nodes). 2) I built a flow routing algorithm. In this method, I integrated techniques from computer vision and machine learning and utilized varied spatial layers such as local landscape patterns, land cover data, and elevation. This innovative approach has markedly enhanced the precision of stream mapping in diverse environments, including open water, wetlands, and engineered terrain. 3) I designed and implemented a stream conflation algorithm that aggregated streams into a higher-level hydrologic unit. This algorithm employed a hybrid raster-vector approach and leveraged spatial indexing tree and hash tables to improve data processing accuracy and efficiency. The data has been released by the USGS (https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/66d72996d34eef5af66ca61b).

Other than the Earth stream mapping, I also mapped valley networks (VNs), which are ancient dry streams on Mars. The global Mars VNs’ mapping results have been published in the Nature Communications.

Hydrology and Fluvial Landform Structure

One aspect of my work is using the fluvial landform hierarchy to understand the influence of environmental factors and the landform evolution process.

When I was a postdoctoral scholar at the University of Illinois Chicago, my work focused on identifying and analyzing the hierarchical structure of depressions on the DEM. Depressions are areas with lower elevation that do not have an outlet on the DEM. These depressions can be natural, such as sinkholes or glacial potholes, or they can result from human activities, such as land subsidence or ponds. In this project, my role was to develop an automated workflow that streamlines the entire process—1) dataset preparation (batch downloading, map projections, and format conversions), 2) data processing (building the binary depression hierarchy and transforming it into a general tree structure, extracting the depression layer from the hierarchy tree), and 3) data analysis (analyzing the relationships between different depression layers and environmental factors, such as land use, lithology, and soil type). The automated workflow allows me to parameterize the entire process and analyze results across different spatial scales and parameters automatically. Furthermore, it ensures a well-structured, repeatable, and reliable workflow, making the project transparent and trackable for debugging.

At NIU, I investigated the optimal channel network (OCN), which is a minimalist model describing fluvial landform evolution to investigate the maturity of fluvial landforms. Previous research suggests that through the landform evolution process, the energy dissipated along an OCN is minimal. That theory provides an opportunity to investigate the fluvial landform’s maturity. I analyzed the maturity of watersheds on Earth. The results suggested that terrestrial watersheds are very mature (with mean maturity reaching 90%), and the associations between watershed maturity and environmental factors are stronger in arid/semi-arid areas than in humid areas. The maturity of Martian watersheds showed that Martian fluvial landforms are, in general, not as mature as their terrestrial counterparts. The results suggest that fluvial erosion processes operated long enough to carve the Martian surface in some regions.

Using Spatial Data Science to Test the Early Mars Climate Hypothesis

The early Mars climatic conditions have been debated for decades. Based on the geomorphological evidence, Mars might be “habitable” and was “warm” enough to allow the liquid water to flow on the surface three billion years ago. However, many climate modelers have encountered difficulties in modeling such early “warm and wet” conditions with an above freezing temperature. To reconcile different views, I used spatial data science technique to investigate geometry properties of VNs, which offered convincing evidence for its past water activities, as my research objects. Specifically, I trained a model using Earth’s geomorphologic and climatic data and used the model to predict the early Mars’ climate conditions.

Under the supervision of my doctoral advisor, Prof. Wei Luo, I developed a spatial association estimator by explicitly considering the effects of distance decay and level of discretization for continuous variables. The new method is called the SPatial Association DEtector (SPADE). My findings are published in the International Journal of Geographic Information Sciences.

With the help of this tool, I discovered that junction angles are the fingerprint of long-term climate conditions. Later, I analogized the frequency of VNs’ junction angles between Earth and Mars to estimate the duration of “warm” Mars. Based on the results, the duration of “warm” Mars could be from ∼4.4 to ∼77 million years, which is long enough to sustain surface water flow to form VNs. I published my findings in the Earth and Planetary Science Letters.
